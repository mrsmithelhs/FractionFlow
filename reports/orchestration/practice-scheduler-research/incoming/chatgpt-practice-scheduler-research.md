# Executive Summary

The evidence on adaptive practice schedulers in math is mixed.  Several controlled studies found **positive learning gains** from adaptive or varied practice: e.g. Roschelle et al. (2016) showed that middle-school students using the ASSISTments online homework system (with teacher-driven adaptivity and feedback) scored significantly higher on end-of-year math tests than peers.  Rau et al. (2014) found that 4th–5th graders using an ITS for fractions learned more when tasks were *interleaved* (mixed representations) rather than *blocked*.  In some Cognitive Tutor Algebra studies, treated students slightly outperformed controls (e.g. Wolfson et al. 2008 saw higher scores on a targeted fractions test).  

However, **several rigorous evaluations found no benefit or even declines**.  A large 2-year RCT of Carnegie Learning’s Cognitive Tutor (grades 8–12) showed *no significant difference* in algebra achievement between adaptive tutoring and business-as-usual teaching.  A quasi-experiment with underachieving 8th graders found that traditional teacher-led instruction **outperformed** ALEKS (an adaptive math tutor) on standardized tests.  In general, no evidence suggests that simply *predicting answers* or spending more time boosts learning; only genuine mastery effects count.  

**Key implication for FractionFlow:** start with very simple adaptivity.  Focus first on known good practices like mixing problem types and offering review, rather than a sophisticated student model. For example, cycle through different fraction representations (bars, number-lines) instead of drilling one format.  Implement a minimal “mastery” rule (e.g. a concept treated as learned after a few correct attempts) and allow learners to request more help or easier tasks.  Pilot test whether these limited adaptations improve retention before adding complexity.  The strongest caveat is that many reported gains come from integrated tutor redesigns and supportive feedback; removal of content or oversimplified adaptivity can hurt motivation.   

**Next steps:**  As a first experiment, try a baseline fixed-sequence practice on one fraction concept (e.g. “find equivalent fractions”) and compare to a version that mixes in varied representations after each success (interleaving).  Measure immediate correctness and short-term retention. Avoid assuming any universal “optimal” success rate or forgetting model.  Learner choice should override the system non-punitively (e.g. permit changing problem type or asking for help without penalty).  (See **Two Directions** below for concrete examples.) 

# Prior-system Case Matrix

- **Cognitive Tutor Algebra I (Wolfson et al., 2008)** – *Quasi-experimental field trial, Grades 9–12 (Carnegie Learning)*.  Students used an early fraction-capable tutor (PUMP+PAT) in class.  Intervention: computer tutor + textbook vs. traditional (textbook only).  Outcome: On a **targeted fraction/multiple-representation test**, tutor students outscored controls (mean 3.54 vs 2.34) with a significant effect (Improvement Index ≈26).  On a general algebra test (Iowa Algebra Aptitude, N≈247) the difference was small (0.50 vs 0.45) and *not* statistically significant.  Learner model: ACT-R/KC-based, mastery-driven.  Assistance: hints and feedback on demand were available; teachers were involved.  Comparators: concurrent classes taught normally.  Duration: 9 months.  Gains were modest and specific; standard algebra outcomes did not improve significantly. (Evidence Tier: mixed; see .)

- **Cognitive Tutor Algebra I (Ritter et al., 2007)** – *Randomized trial, Grade 9, N≈255 (Carnegie Learning)*.  Intervention: half-year Cognitive Tutor vs. business-as-usual.  Outcome: After 9 months, tutor students scored higher on an ETS End-of-Course Algebra test (mean ≈17.4 vs 15.3).  WWC rated this as a “substantively important positive effect” (though p-value was marginal).  Learner model: same ITS model with Knowledge Components.  No difference analysis beyond reported means.  (Meets WWC standards with reservations.)

- **ALEKS in Support Class (Khazanchi et al., 2023)** – *Quasi-experimental pre-post, underachieving 8th-grade math support*.  Intervention: ALEKS ITS supplementation vs. traditional teacher-led remediation.  Population: 158 students (60 control, 98 ALEKS).  Outcome: Both groups improved statistically, but post-test scores were significantly higher for **teacher-led** than ALEKS, with lower variance.  Five-year trend data also favored traditional instruction.  (Design: year-over-year cohort comparison, not randomized.)  Conclusion: ALEKS did *not* improve outcomes beyond regular teaching; teachers did better.  (Statistical tests showed teacher-led > ALEKS.)  

- **ASSISTments Homework (Roschelle et al., 2016)** – *Cluster RCT, Grade 7 in Maine, N=2850*.  Intervention: teachers used ASSISTments (adaptive online homework with immediate feedback + reports) vs. existing homework practices.  Learners: 43 schools randomized (23 treatment, 22 control).  Outcome: End-of-year math scores (TerraNova Level 17) were significantly higher for ASSISTments students (mean 690.79 vs. 681.95, p<.05).  Effect = +7 percentile points (improvement index 7).  Benefit was largest for lower-achieving students.  No evidence of negative effects or increased attrition.  (Rated Tier 1 **strong evidence** by WWC.)  ASSISTments gave immediate correctness feedback and let teachers assign new problems based on the data.  

- **Interleaved Practice in Fractions (Rau et al., 2014)** – *Classroom AIED experiment, Grade 4–5, N=230*.  Intervention: fraction addition/subtraction problems with multiple graphical representations (pie, bar, number line).  Four scheduling conditions: blocked (all pie then all bar, etc.) vs. moderately or fully interleaved.  Outcome: Interleaved practice led to **higher post-test learning** than blocked.  A Bayesian Knowledge Tracing analysis of log data confirmed higher *learning rates* under interleaving.  Short-term practice performance was worse (more errors) during interleaving, but retention was better.  Learners received model-traced feedback and hints as usual.  The study isolated scheduling: content and tutor interfaces were constant, only the sequence differed.  

- **Cognitive Tutor (Pane et al., 2014)** – *Large-scale RCT, Grades 8–12, N≈5700*.  Intervention: two-year Cognitive Tutor Algebra I curriculum (classroom + tutor 2 days/week) vs. business-as-usual algebra.  Students: ~5,700, multi-state.  Outcome: After 1 and 2 years, algebra test scores were **no different** between groups (difference <1 percentile).  WWC: “no statistically significant effect”.  (Improvement Index ≈0 after 2 years.)  Suggests full algebra tutoring yielded little advantage in this context.  

*Sources:* Wolfson et al. (2008); Ritter et al. (2007); Khazanchi et al. (2023); Roschelle et al. (2016); Rau et al. (2014); Pane et al. (2014).  

# Findings by Question

## 1. What has been tried? (Cases)  
**Positive/Adaptive cases:** ASSISTments (Roschelle 2016) reported significant learning gains [E].  ITS with **interleaved variety** (Rau 2014) also boosted fraction learning [E].  Some Cognitive Tutor studies found small positive effects [E] (Wolfson 2008; Ritter 2007).  These involved model-tracing tutors with just-in-time feedback and allowing either hints or multiple attempts. 

**Null/Negative cases:** Large controlled trials of adaptive algebra curricula often found *no gains* (Pane 2014 [E]).  In a quasi-experiment ALEKS underperformed teacher-led support [E].  In cases of mismatch or low prior knowledge, ITS can yield poor motivation or lower achievement [T].  Across cases, adaptivity was often bundled with new content or pedagogy, so isolating the scheduling effect is hard.  Some studies report reduced motivation (especially due to extrinsic rewards).  No study specifically evaluated an entirely client-side, no-login fraction tutor like FractionFlow; evidence from other architectures must be applied cautiously. 

## 2. Learner-Model Options  
Most ITS use *Knowledge-Component mastery models* or simple correctness counters.  Under sparse data, heavy models (Bayesian Knowledge Tracing, Deep KT) risk overfitting.  A simple local rule (e.g. mark a fraction skill “mastered” after N consecutive correct solves) is explainable and easy to implement.  This must handle slips/guesses: allowing multiple attempts, ignoring stray wrong answers, and adjusting if later errors appear.  For example, Riley et al. found modeling students’ latent learning rates useful.  But an overly strict model can freeze out practice; conversely, treating first success as mastery is unsafe (it may reflect guessing or assistant use).  We should avoid turning one mistake into a permanent zero or a single correct into full mastery.  Without history (FractionFlow’s limited log), emphasize recent performance and offer a clear “review later” path.  In short, a rule-based counter plus coarse uncertainty (e.g. require 2–3 independent correct tries after hints) is justified by practice learning, whereas a full probabilistic trace is not supported by direct evidence here. 

## 3. Scheduling Challenge, Support, Practice  
Evidence supports *interleaving and variation*: mixing problem types or representations (see Rau 2014) helps learning [E].  By contrast, purely blocked drills (all problems of one form first) were less effective [E].  Spacing out reviews over multiple visits strongly improves retention in theory, but FractionFlow has no cross-day memory, so we can only pseudo-space within a session (e.g. revisit a concept after a gap of other problems).  Worked examples or scaffolded problems are often used for initial concept teaching; once partial skill is gained, increase difficulty (larger denominators, less guidance).  Very little adaptive policy research pinpoints an “optimal” success rate or mastery threshold in math practice—each study sets its own rule (often 90–95% accuracy).  We should not assume a universal target.  Instead, we might adapt difficulty per task (per item or concept): e.g. after a correct answer with minimal help, present a slightly harder version; after a struggle, repeat or offer more support.  Unproductive struggle (many errors on same step) should trigger hints or a simpler analog task.  Repeated remediation (if a student never succeeds) may indicate a missing prerequisite; if so, consider branching to review a supporting skill.  Since FractionFlow cannot measure elapsed time or forgetting, we focus on within-session logic.  The minimal data needed is simply recent correctness counts per concept (available in current log).  In summary: schedule variety and periodic review [E], fade support gradually [D/H], but be wary of fixed numeric thresholds (no universal evidence).  

## 4. Learner Control and Agency  
Users must be allowed to change pace or mode without penalty.  Systems that *strictly enforce* an algorithm often frustrate learners.  Design experience suggests: let students request easier or harder problems, and allow skipping or revisiting topics.  In ASSISTments, teachers could reassign problems based on reports; similarly, FractionFlow should not force a learner along a hidden path.  Ideally, a request for “more practice” or “show me how” should not count as failure.  Novice learners may not know their own needs: they often prefer practice they find familiar.  Therefore, we should track such choices (e.g. repeated skipping) and respond (perhaps by interjecting a motivation or mini-explanation).  Overriding the scheduler should only slow progress gracefully, not “punish” the user with harder tasks.  Evidence specific to overrides is scarce, but studies of self-regulated learning suggest novices benefit from scaffolding rather than full choice.  In practice, offer a transparent control (e.g. “want an easier example?” button) and treat it as additional data (no change in mastery count).  Watch for student frustration: if many hints or repeats are requested, the rule might be too hard.

# Two Feasible First Directions

1. **Baseline (Non-adaptive) sequence, one concept:** For example, practice adding fractions with common denominators using bar models. Present a fixed set of problems (e.g. 1/5+2/5, 2/5+1/5, 3/5+2/5) in a row.  Do not adapt based on correctness; provide static hints only if student requests.  *Example:* Alice solves 1/5+2/5 (gets it wrong), she may try again or ask for a hint (“draw bars”). No matter what, problem 2 and 3 are the same (pre-chosen).  Next opportunity after her error does **not** change in difficulty or representation. This establishes a baseline for learning and engagement without any scheduler beyond the fixed script.

2. **Simple adaptive rule – “mastery + scaffold adjustment” on a small skill:** Focus on one small task recipe, say “place a fraction on a number line.”  Start with an easy fraction (e.g. 1/2). If the student answers it correctly on the first try (no hints), then give a slightly harder one (e.g. 3/4). If the student gets it wrong, immediately give a simpler scaffold: e.g. break the problem into steps or switch to a fraction bar model. After assistance, offer a second attempt at the original problem. *Example flow:* Bob correctly places 1/2 on the line, so next we ask 3/4. If he fails 3/4, we then provide a hint (“1/2 is here; 3/4 is larger”) and ask 3/4 again. We do **not** let one success conclude mastery fully, nor do we finalize failure after a single mistake. This small rule set can be coded locally: track “streak” of correctness on this task, and on error trigger one guided retry instead of moving on. *Important not-to-infer:* A single error isn’t treated as catastrophic failure—just a signal to review. Likewise, two successes don’t imply “done forever” (we would need more evidence to call it mastered). We also do **not** enforce a numeric success rate; we just respond case-by-case. This concrete scaffolding example shows how the *next* problem changes based on error or correctness, without any backend or credentials.  

# Source Ledger and Gaps

- **Rau et al. (2014), _Int. J. Artif. Intell. Ed._ 24(2):125–161.** (Full text) Controlled classroom experiment on fraction representations. Found interleaved scheduling improved learning. *Limitation:* focused on multiple graphical reps; no long-term retention measure. **Access:** DOI 10.1007/s40593-013-0011-7 (Springer).
- **Roschelle et al. (2016), _AERA Open_ 2(4):** (Full text) Cluster RCT of ASSISTments (online homework) with 7th graders. Found a small but significant end-of-year math gain (improvement index ~7). *Design note:* this was a complex intervention (teacher training + software). **Access:** AERA Open 2016, doi 10.1177/2332858416653483.
- **Pane et al. (2014), _Educ. Eval. & Policy Anal._ 36(2):127–144.** (Full text via ERIC) Two-year RCT of Cognitive Tutor vs. standard algebra. No effect on standardized algebra test. *Representative of large-scale deployment.* **Access:** EJ1024233 (ERIC).
- **Ritter et al. (2007), _Supporting Learning Flow_ (IOS Press).** (Full text via WWC) Randomized trial of Cognitive Tutor Algebra (9th grade, ~255 students). Tutor mean=17.41 vs BAU 15.28 on algebra post-test. *WWC rated “substantively important effect” (not sig).* **Access:** WWC Std Review, Study ID 82107.
- **Wolfson et al. (2008), Carnegie Learning report.** (Full text via WWC) Quasi-experiment (Grades 9–12, Pittsburgh) of early Cognitive Tutor. Significant gain on a custom multiple-representations test. *Met WWC standards with reservations.* **Access:** WWC study 82093.
- **Khazanchi et al. (2023), *Computers in Math & Sci Teaching* 42(2):155–176.** (Summary via EduTec article) Quasi-experiment: ALEKS tutoring vs. teacher-led for underachieving 8th graders. Teacher instruction produced higher scores. *Not peer-reviewed full text in our sources; summary cited.* **Access:** LearnTechLib (preprint news summary).

Progressive Educ._ 22(1):14–30.** (Systematic review) Reports that most ITS studies show achievement and motivation gains, but notes some cases of *decreased* motivation (due to extrinsic rewards). *Provides context, not a primary study.* **Access:** IJPE (open).

**Gaps and Unanswered Questions:** There is very little published data on adaptive practice specifically for fractions (most ITS work targets broader algebra).  Few studies isolate *scheduling* from other features (feedback, curriculum).  We found no study of learner override or self-pacing features in ITS.  Adult or certification-prep contexts were not represented in accessible evaluations.  Also, nearly all evidence addresses *immediate or end-of-course tests*; effects on long-term retention or transfer were not measured.  Key questions likely require fresh user testing in the actual fraction app rather than more papers.  For example, “Does allowing students to skip to harder problems improve or hurt learning?” and “What minimal mastery threshold aligns with true understanding?” remain open in FractionFlow’s context.
