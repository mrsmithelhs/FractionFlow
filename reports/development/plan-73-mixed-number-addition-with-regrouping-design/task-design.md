# Plan 73 — Mixed-number addition with regrouping: design comparison

**Recommendation for owner review, not an adopted design or implementation.** On source baseline `a0bae8d1d93509786347a02fc7b01232d378d894`, recommend the single final response as the first slice, with optional non-answering unit help. The exchange is a plausible later comparison, but its extra required step earns its cost only if the product needs evidence of the learner's explicit grouping decision, or learner observation shows that making it improves understanding enough to justify the burden. Neither has been established here. Both designs require the learner to determine the final counts; only the second additionally observes a proposed grouping. No learner or rendered evaluation of either proposal was performed.

## Common task and boundary

First slice: positive mixed operands with positive proper fractional parts, a shared denominator within each item, and fractional parts totaling at least one but less than two wholes. Each item therefore permits exactly one new whole. Preserve the source denominator, without requiring simplification. Two proposed first-batch items, ordered zero then nonzero, are the exact packet examples; the tiny batch is a design starting point, not a sufficient practice dose or efficacy claim. A separately approved implementation must define authored identities, denominator and count limits, and admission.

| Case | Prompt | Original pieces after pooling | Equivalent grouped total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zero | 1 3/8 + 2 5/8 | 3 wholes and 8 eighths | 4 wholes and 0 eighths; display **4** |
| Nonzero | 1 5/8 + 2 6/8 | 3 wholes and 11 eighths | 4 wholes and 3 eighths; display **4 3/8** |

The count pairs above describe reasoning, not prefilled initial answers. [Exact evidence](exact-evidence.mjs), run with `node reports/development/plan-73-mixed-number-addition-with-regrouping-design/exact-evidence.mjs`, uses `addMixedNumbers`, exact fraction addition/comparison, and equality of ungrouped and grouped values. It checks both examples, an unequal witness for each, and `1 3/8 + 2 4/8 = 3 7/8`, whose seven pieces are below one whole and must never offer an exchange. It checks mathematical value and the boundary, not UI behavior or pedagogy.

Both candidates initially show the same original operands as grouped stacked mixed notation and equal-scale models, with coherent accessible names. They provide the same optional unit help: an unfilled eighths whole and “How many eighths make one whole? Look at the whole units and the eighth pieces in both amounts.” It supplies neither pooled nor final counts. More/Less should truthfully offer the same aid unless a separately designed difference exists. Keyboard and linear access are available in either setting. The learner can close help and retain the draft; help use belongs to the attempt and survives retry and same-attempt reconstruction.

## Candidate A: one final response

Prompt: “Add the amounts. Enter the whole units and remaining eighth pieces in the sum.” Two initially blank fields, **Whole units in the sum** and **Remaining eighth pieces**, have a fixed `/ 8` suffix for the second, whose accessible name includes the unit. One Check evaluates the pair. A blank means not entered, never zero. A learner must explicitly enter `0` for a zero remainder; accepting whole-only **4** refers to the app's displayed result after `4`, `0` are checked, not to treating a blank field as zero. Nonzero acceptance displays app-supplied grouped stacked **4 3/8**, preserving eighths, with attribution that the learner supplied the counts and the app formatted the numeral.

| Journey | Learner action and check | What feedback can show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zero | From `1 3/8 + 2 5/8`, learner determines `4` wholes and `0` eighths; Check accepts. | The accepted settled model shows the same 3 original whole units plus eight original eighth pieces arranged into a fourth equal-scale whole, with 0 eighths left. State “8 eighths make 1 whole; 4 wholes, 0 eighths.” Display 4. |
| Nonzero | From `1 5/8 + 2 6/8`, learner determines `4` and `3`; Check accepts. | The accepted model groups eight of the eleven original pieces into one whole and retains three pieces: “8 eighths make 1 whole; 4 wholes, 3 eighths.” Display 4 3/8. |

Feedback after acceptance can illustrate an equivalence; it cannot establish that the learner used it. The learner may have counted, used an algorithm, guessed, or had outside help. The app observes entries, checks, and recorded help, not strategy or independent understanding. Before acceptance do not expose pooled totals, grouped answer, hidden solution model, or answer-bearing accessibility labels.

## Candidate B: a learner-controlled exchange

After the same prompt and aids, ask the learner to establish the pooled quantities in editable fields: whole units and eighth pieces. Check these quantities; a wrong pair receives neutral retry and remains editable, with no answer supplied. Once established, show the learner-established 3 wholes and 8 or 11 pieces, keeping source pieces identifiable. Ask: “Choose eighth pieces to make one whole. How many eighth pieces are left?” The learner chooses exactly eight individual pieces and enters an explicit remainder count, including `0`. Selecting a piece toggles its membership, so selections can be undone; a separate exchange Check validates the selected count and remainder against the established pool. There is no preselected group or prefilled remainder. Both operands' pieces are equivalent units; any eight of the pooled pieces qualify. The original three whole units remain visible. A wrong group size or remainder prompts “Make one complete whole and account for every remaining piece”; retain all selections and the entered remainder for revision, without showing the correct counts. Help shows an unfilled eighth-part unit and asks the same unit question, not the answer.

Once valid, replace the selected eight pieces with one same-scale whole and keep all unselected pieces visible. State the resulting quantities from the checked exchange. Then the learner enters the final two fields and checks; the exchange result is visible and thus supports, and can reveal, the final counts. This is deliberate answer leakage in exchange for observing the grouping decision; the final check is not independent evidence of calculating those counts. A learner can return to revise the pooled counts or selection before final acceptance; doing so invalidates the exchange and final feedback, retains recoverable drafts where consistent, and requires rechecking affected steps. A fresh attempt resets state; same-attempt replay preserves edits, selections, checks, help and their order, without silently claiming a closed help panel was never used.

| Journey | Learner-controlled decisions | Settled exchange and accepted result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zero | Establish `3` and `8`; select all eight source pieces and enter `0` remaining; check exchange. Enter `4`, `0` and check final response. | Three existing wholes plus the new whole, no loose eighths. Display 4. |
| Nonzero | Establish `3` and `11`; select any eight source pieces and enter `3` remaining; check exchange. Enter `4`, `3` and check final response. | Three existing wholes plus the new whole, three original eighths left. Display 4 3/8. |

The exchange checks a selection of a full unit and an accounting of the remainder; it still cannot prove the learner understands equivalence, selected unaided, or can transfer. It adds pooled-count establishment, selection and remainder entry, exchange checking, and a final response, plus state, recovery and focus complexity. It is more than a confirmation click, but it may feel repetitive, especially when the exchange output already displays the final quantities. Its actual benefit and cost require learner observation.

## Checking and recovery for both

The prospective checker first handles missing, malformed and out-of-range inputs with neutral field-specific guidance; it does not score or replace drafts. For valid counts, compare the exact mixed value before judging organization. If unequal, say “Check the amount and try again.” If equal but the piece count is at least the denominator, say “Your amount is the same. Group enough pieces to make a whole, then check again.” For example `3` and `8` in the zero case and `3` and `11` in the nonzero case have the correct value but are ungrouped. If admitted, both must reach organization guidance, not an unequal-sum error. The future field bounds must admit these witnesses or explicitly explain a narrower range before value classification; a design that promises this feedback cannot hide them behind a limit of eight. `4` and `1` for the zero case is unequal. Editing either final field clears stale feedback and preserves the other; Check uses the current revision. For B, analogous validation retains the learner's selections and entries on retry. Avoid giving final counts in help or error messages.

## Equivalent visual and linear paths

All wholes use the same width and unit scale. In A's accepted visualization, original pieces are traceable as a group of eight plus any remainder; exchange does not create or delete pieces or alter the unit. A textual settled summary gives all quantities and the same equivalence. In B the visual path presents individually selectable, numbered source pieces with keyboard-operable toggle controls, selected state exposed without reliance on color, a count of selected pieces, and an editable remainder. The linear path presents the same numbered pieces as a list of labeled toggles, the same selection count and remainder field, Check, feedback and revision. It does not replace the learner's selection with an already solved statement. Both can select any eight, account for every other piece, and inspect the same pre- and post-exchange quantities. Switching paths preserves the same draft and decision state. Non-drag input is available throughout. Optional animation may illustrate the checked exchange; reduced motion or skipping animation immediately shows the identical settled state. Announce one concise feedback target without duplicate spoken fragments; maintain focus on edit, return focus from Help, and offer clear keyboard order. Actual assistive-technology speech needs later testing.

Stack each mixed numeral as a single grouped object with one accessible name, retaining source denominator. Label originals, selected pieces, unselected pieces, original wholes and the new whole so that color and spatial position are not the only distinctions. A complete fallback summary must include original whole count, total source-piece count, selected/exchanged count, new whole count, remaining piece count, and resulting total whole count when those states are established; distinguish unknown or not entered from zero. Never substitute a partial summary for an unreadable graphic.

These are prior browser observations, not proof of this design. Eleven pieces, a new whole, individual controls and intermediate summaries can increase height and create cramped targets or overflow; a count-only fallback cannot replace B's selectable pieces without an equivalent selection mechanism.

A future implementation must capture its own rendered checks at 360 × 740, normal and 200% root text, for both cases: initial, intermediate exchange if B is chosen, partial/zero, retry and help, and acceptance. Check full-width overflow, readable fixed suffix and stacked numerals, stable equal unit scale, complete summaries, selection targets, focus/caret, path switching, keyboard operation and optional/reduced motion. Include a non-drag path. CSS root scaling is not actual browser zoom; physical devices, real assistive-technology speech and learner understanding remain separate checks.
